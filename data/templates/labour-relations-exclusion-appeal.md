# Appeal Template: Challenging Labour Relations Exclusion

## 📋 Template Information

**Use this template when:** Your mental stress/chronic stress claim was denied because the WCB/WSIB/WCAT said it was caused by "employer decisions" (termination, discipline, work evaluation) and is therefore excluded under the "labour relations exclusion" or "decision of employer" rule.

**Jurisdiction:** Primarily BC (Workers Compensation Act s.135(1)(c)), but adaptable to other provinces with similar exclusions.

**Legal basis:** *Pickering v. Workers' Compensation Board*, 2025 BCSC 376 - BC Supreme Court ruled the labour relations exclusion should be "read down" (limited) to only apply to generic management processes taken in good faith.

**Before using:** Read the [Claim Suppression & Employer Retaliation](../knowledge-base/claim-suppression-retaliation.md) guide to understand the legal arguments.

### LEGAL ARGUMENTS

#### 1. The Labour Relations Exclusion Should Be Read Down (*Pickering v. WCB*)

In *Pickering v. Workers' Compensation Board*, 2025 BCSC 376, the BC Supreme Court ruled that the labour relations exclusion is **arbitrary** if applied broadly and should be **"read down"** (interpreted narrowly).

The Court held at para. 404-407:

> "[404] In my opinion, the Labour Relations Exclusion should be read down.
>
> [407] Hence, the Labour Relations Exclusion (s. 135(1)(c)) should be limited to management decisions on **generic processes** and actions taken in **good faith**."

**Application to my case:**

The *Pickering* decision establishes a two-part test. The exclusion should only apply if:
1. The employer's action was a **generic process** (normal business operation), AND
2. The action was taken in **good faith** (honest, legitimate business purpose)

If either element is absent, the exclusion should not apply.

#### 2. My Employer's Actions Were NOT Generic Processes

*Argue that the termination/discipline was targeted retaliation, not a generic business process.*

My employer's [termination/discipline/evaluation] was **not** a generic management process. It was **targeted retaliation** for exercising my statutory right to file a workers' compensation claim.

**Evidence this was targeted, not generic:**

[INCLUDE SPECIFIC FACTS SHOWING TARGETING:]
- Timeline: Action occurred [X] days after filing claim
- No prior issues: [X] years of employment with no prior discipline
- Pretextual reasons: Stated reasons for termination contradict my documented performance record
- Pattern: Other workers who filed claims experienced similar treatment [if applicable]

**Example:**
"- **Timeline:** I was terminated exactly 23 days after filing my WCB claim, following 7 years of satisfactory employment.

- **No Prior Issues:** I had never received any disciplinary action, written warnings, or negative performance reviews in 7 years. My last annual review (January 2024—two months before my injury) rated me 'exceeds expectations.'

- **Pretextual Reasons:** The termination letter cites 'performance issues' but provides no specific examples and contradicts my documented performance record (attached as Exhibit F: Performance Reviews 2020-2024).

- **Pattern:** A coworker, [NAME], was also terminated within weeks of filing a WCB claim in 2023. This suggests a pattern of retaliation, not generic business decisions."

Generic processes are things like company-wide restructuring, documented performance management systems applied equally to all employees, or layoffs due to genuine business downturns. My termination was none of these—it was retaliatory action against me specifically for filing a claim.

#### 3. My Employer's Actions Were NOT Taken in Good Faith

*Argue that employer acted with retaliatory intent, not honest business purpose.*

My employer's actions were **not** taken in good faith. They were taken in **bad faith** with the intent to punish me for filing a workers' compensation claim and to deter other workers from filing claims.

**Evidence of bad faith:**

[INCLUDE FACTS SHOWING BAD FAITH:]
- Coercion: Employer pressured me to withdraw claim
- Intimidation: Threats of job loss if claim pursued  
- Changed treatment: Immediate shift in attitude after claim filed
- Privacy violations: Attempted to violate privacy protections
- Pretextual justifications: Stated reasons don't match facts

**Example:**
"- **Coercion:** My supervisor explicitly stated on March 18, 2024: 'You should withdraw that WCB claim because it's going to look bad for the department.' This is documented in my contemporaneous notes (Exhibit G) and witnessed by coworker [NAME] who has provided a statement (Exhibit H).

- **Intimidation:** On March 30, 2024, HR manager [NAME] told me: 'Workers who file claims are often not a good fit for this company anymore.' This constitutes an economic threat designed to deprive me of free choice in exercising my statutory rights (*WCAT A2002265*).

- **Changed Treatment:** Before my claim: 7 years of positive reviews, no discipline, preferred shifts, open communication. After my claim: written warning, shift demotion, cold shoulder from management, termination. The only variable that changed was filing the claim.

- **Privacy Violations:** Employer attempted to force me to submit medical information directly to third-party assessor in violation of *Rehn Enterprises* privacy protections. This demonstrates disregard for my legal rights.

- **Pretextual Justifications:** Termination letter cites 'performance issues' that (1) were never raised with me during employment, (2) contradict my documented performance record, and (3) only appeared as justification after I refused to withdraw my claim."

Good faith requires honest, legitimate business purpose. Bad faith is acting with improper motive—here, retaliation and deterrence. The evidence overwhelmingly demonstrates bad faith.

#### 4. Employer Conduct Crossed the Line into Harassment and Bullying

*Argue that conduct was "extremely egregious" and therefore not protected even under traditional interpretation of exclusion.*

Even under the traditional interpretation of the labour relations exclusion, employer conduct that crosses the line into harassment and bullying is not protected.

In *WCAT-2013-03061* (Nov 1, 2013), the tribunal held:

> "In order for an employer's conduct to cross that line and be viewed as stepping out of the 'labour relations exclusion' and into harassment and bullying, it must be **extremely egregious behaviour**, such that a reasonable person would clearly see it as abusive or personally threatening."

**Application to my case:**

My employer's conduct meets the "extremely egregious" standard:

[DESCRIBE THE MOST EGREGIOUS ACTIONS:]
- [Action 1]: [Why this is abusive/threatening]
- [Action 2]: [Why this is abusive/threatening]
- [Action 3]: [Why this is abusive/threatening]

**Example:**
"- **Threat of Job Loss:** HR manager telling me 'Workers who file claims are often not a good fit' is a direct economic threat. A reasonable person would view this as personally threatening—my livelihood was being held hostage to force me to abandon my statutory rights.

- **Isolation and Humiliation:** After my claim was filed, I was publicly removed from my long-held day shift position in front of coworkers, with no explanation given. This public demotion was humiliating and clearly designed to shame me for filing my claim.

- **Coercive Pressure:** Supervisor's repeated statements that my claim 'would look bad' combined with sudden discipline after 7 years of no issues constitutes a campaign of coercion to deprive me of free choice in exercising my rights (definition of intimidation per *WCAT A2002265*).

- **Cumulative Effect:** Taken together—the threats, public demotion, sudden discipline, cold shoulder, privacy violations, and ultimate termination—this constitutes a pattern of abusive behaviour designed to punish and intimidate. A reasonable person would clearly see this as personally threatening, not merely poor management."

This is not a case of a poorly communicated performance review or a supervisor being unprofessional. This is systematic abuse designed to crush a worker who dared to exercise statutory rights.

#### 5. Charter Rights Require Reading Down the Exclusion

*Argue that applying the exclusion violates your Charter rights.*

The Supreme Court of Canada has held that tribunals have jurisdiction to consider incidental Charter questions when interpreting their governing statutes (*R. v. 974649 Ontario Inc.* (Dunedin), 2001 SCC 81).

In *Caron v. Oshawa* (2001 SCC, File 36605), the SCC instructed tribunals:

> "This Court's decision should be interpreted by workers' compensation boards and tribunals across the country as an indication that they need to fulfill their statutory role in return to work in keeping with fundamental human rights principles."

**Application to my case:**

Applying the labour relations exclusion to deny my claim violates:

1. **Section 15 Charter rights to equality:** The exclusion creates a two-tier system where workers with physical injuries get full protection, but workers with mental injuries caused by employer retaliation are denied protection. This discriminates on the basis of disability (mental vs. physical).

2. **Section 7 Charter rights to security of the person:** Denying compensation for employer-caused mental stress while simultaneously preventing me from suing my employer (due to no-fault protection) deprives me of security of the person in a manner not in keeping with principles of fundamental justice.

3. **Dignity interests protected by human rights codes:** Employer retaliation violates my human right to file a workers' compensation claim without reprisal. Allowing the exclusion to shield this retaliation from accountability undermines my dignity and the remedial purpose of workers' compensation.

The *Pickering* "read down" approach reconciles the labour relations exclusion with Charter values. I request the tribunal apply that approach here.
